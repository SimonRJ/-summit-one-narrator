T17.2

Use counterexamples and contradictions carefully

Disprove with a legal counterexample; prove impossibility by contradiction.

2:03 · Full HD

Try it in your notebook

Seven cups are upright; each move turns over exactly two. Can all seven end upside down?

Check your reasoning

Each move turns two upright cups down, two upside-down cups up, or one of each. The number upright therefore changes by −2, +2 or 0. Starting at 7, it stays odd and can never reach 0. There is always at least one upright cup.

Read the transcript

0:00 Distinguish a counterexample from a contradiction

A counterexample is one case that breaks a claim about everything. A proof by contradiction starts with a supposition and follows what it would imply. If it leads to two facts that cannot both hold, the supposition fails.

0:15 Choose evidence for a possibility claim

Competitions often ask whether something is possible. If it is, show it. If it is not, you need an argument, and contradiction is the standard way to build one.

0:26 Test the claim that multiplication increases a number

A student says: multiplying always makes a number bigger. Five times three is fifteen, which is bigger than five. Is the claim true? Pause and hunt for a case that breaks it.

0:40 Try multiplying six by a half, one and zero

Six times one half is three, which is smaller. Six times one is still six. Six times zero is zero. Any one of these is a counterexample, so the claim is false.

0:51 Repair the claim by stating its conditions

A counterexample must obey the claim's own conditions. If the claim were about multiplying a positive number by a number greater than one, then a half and zero would not be allowed, and the repaired claim would be true. Failed claims often become true once the conditions are stated properly.

1:10 Flip pairs of coins starting from nine heads

Now a contradiction. Nine coins all show heads. Each move flips exactly two coins. Can all nine show tails? Suppose it could be done. Each move changes the number of heads by two, or not at all. So the number of heads stays odd for ever.

1:26 Compare the starting and target numbers of heads

It starts at nine, which is odd. All tails would mean zero heads, which is even. An odd number can never become zero this way. Our supposition has led to something impossible, so the task cannot be done.

1:41 Decide whether seven cups can all turn upside down

A counterexample is one allowed case that breaks a claim. A contradiction supposes something is possible and shows that leads to nonsense. Notebook challenge: seven cups stand upright. Each move turns over exactly two cups. Can all seven end upside down?
