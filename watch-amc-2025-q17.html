The number pyramid

How does moving just two numbers change the top of the pyramid?

Open MP4 directlyReturn to Test Centre

Pause when the question ends at 0:22. Trace one number from the bottom row to the top before arranging the other four.

Read the question and narration

Xian fills this grid with numbers. He places the numbers from 1 to 5 in the 5 squares in the bottom row, in some order. In the next two rows, each square contains the sum of the numbers in the 3 closest squares in the row below. The arrows indicate how this addition works for the first square in the second row. What is the largest possible value of the number in the top square?

(A) 27 · (B) 29 · (C) 32 · (D) 34 · (E) 35

This is a maximising problem. We will count how often each bottom number contributes.

The centre counts three times, its neighbours twice, and the ends once.

Put five centrally, three and four beside it, and one and two at the ends.

Larger numbers in more-counted positions give a larger total.

The middle sums are nine, twelve, eleven. The maximum is thirty-two: answer C.

Read the solution

The top counts the five bottom positions 1, 2, 3, 2 and 1 times. Put 5 in the centre, 3 and 4 in the neighbouring positions, and 1 and 2 at the ends. One arrangement is 1, 3, 5, 4, 2. Its middle row is 9, 12, 11, so the top is 32 — answer C.

Why is this the maximum? If a bigger number is in a position counted fewer times than a smaller number, swapping them increases the total. Keep the biggest numbers in the most heavily counted positions; then no swap can improve it.

Can two different rows tie?

Keep the numbers 1 to 5. How many different bottom rows give the same maximum? Count a row and its reversal separately.

Check your count

There are four. Keep 5 in the centre. The 3 and 4 can trade places next to it, and the 1 and 2 can trade ends. Both swaps preserve each number’s contribution. The two independent choices give 2 × 2 = 4 rows.
