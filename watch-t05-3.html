T05.3

Prove that the classification is reversible

Check a counting method by giving every outcome exactly one address.

2:00 · Full HD

Try it in your notebook

Four different books stand in a row. Two particular books must be kept apart. How many arrangements are possible?

Check your reasoning

There are 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24 orders altogether. Temporarily join the two specified books into a block. The block and the other books have 6 orders, with 2 orders inside the block. Removing those 12 adjacent arrangements leaves 12.

Read the transcript

A counting method is correct when every outcome has exactly one address. From the outcome you can tell which choices made it, and every set of choices makes a real outcome. If both are true, nothing is missed and nothing is counted twice.

Double counting and missed cases are invisible if you only look at the final number. Checking the addresses is the way to trust a count without redoing it.

Five different students sit in a row of five seats. Three are girls and two are boys. The two boys must not sit next to each other. In how many ways can they be seated? Pause and look for a way to build every legal row exactly once.

Seat the girls first. Three girls can be arranged in six orders. Around them are four gaps: one at each end and two between.

Put each boy into a different gap. The first boy has four gaps to choose from, and the second has three. That is twelve ways, so there are six times twelve, seventy-two seatings.

Now check the addresses. Take any legal row. Remove the boys and you see the girls' order. Look where the boys were and you see which gaps they used. So each legal row comes from exactly one set of choices. The count is complete and has no repeats.

A second method confirms it. Five people can sit in one hundred and twenty ways. Glue the boys together as one block: four objects in twenty-four orders, times two for the boys swapping. That is forty-eight unwanted rows. One hundred and twenty minus forty-eight is seventy-two.

Ask two questions of any counting method. Can I recover the choices from every legal outcome? And does every choice give a legal outcome? Notebook challenge: in how many ways can four different books stand on a shelf if two particular books must not be next to each other?
