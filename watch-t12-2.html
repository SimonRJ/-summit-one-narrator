T12.2

Connect angles on a circle to the arcs they see

Use equal spacing on a circle, and the link between an angle at the centre and one on the circle.

2:17 · Full HD

Try it in your notebook

On a clock face, what is the smaller angle at the centre between the hour marks labelled 2 and 7?

Check your reasoning

Twelve equal gaps divide a full turn, so each gap is 360° ÷ 12 = 30°. The shorter route from 2 to 7 crosses five gaps, giving 150°.

Read the transcript

Put twelve points equally spaced round a circle, like the hours on a clock. From the centre, the full turn of three hundred and sixty degrees is shared into twelve equal angles of thirty degrees. Counting gaps between points tells you an angle at the centre.

Clock problems, wheels and regular shapes all use this idea. And there is a second fact, about angles drawn from a point on the circle itself, that unlocks many harder problems.

On the clock circle, join the centre to the point at twelve and to the point at four. What angle is made at the centre? Pause and count carefully.

From twelve to four there are four gaps. Four times thirty is one hundred and twenty degrees. Count the gaps, not the points. There are five points from twelve to four, but only four gaps.

Now stand at a point on the circle instead, at eight, and look at the same arc from twelve to four. The angle you see is exactly half the angle at the centre: sixty degrees. An angle on the circle is half the angle at the centre standing on the same arc.

Why half here? Draw the radius to eight. It splits the angle at eight into two parts, a and b. Since the radii are equal, the two small triangles have equal base angles. Their angles at the centre are one hundred and eighty minus two a, and one hundred and eighty minus two b. Subtract both from a full turn. The centre angle over the arc is two a plus two b, twice the whole angle at eight.

A special case is worth remembering. If the arc is exactly half the circle, the angle at the centre is one hundred and eighty, so the angle on the circle is ninety. Any triangle drawn with a diameter as one side, and its third corner on the circle, has a right angle.

Share three hundred and sixty among the gaps. Count gaps, not points. The angle on the circle is half the angle at the centre. Notebook challenge: on the clock circle, what is the angle at the centre between the points at two and seven?
