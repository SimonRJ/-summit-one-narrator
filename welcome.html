Read the introduction

Welcome to Summit One. A huge number, a hidden pattern, a puzzling shape: each is an invitation to think. Here you'll build the habits and methods to tackle unfamiliar Australian Mathematics Competition problems with confidence.

Upper Primary is the AMC division for Years Five and Six. You have sixty minutes for thirty questions, on paper or online. The questions generally become more challenging as you go.

Questions one to twenty-five are multiple choice: choose one answer from A to E. The final five need a whole number from zero to nine hundred and ninety-nine. You supply the number.

Marks rise through the paper: three each for the first ten, four for the next ten, then five each. The final five are worth six, seven, eight, nine and ten marks. That's one hundred and thirty-five altogether.

Wrong answers do not lose marks. Start with questions you can solve, then return to tougher ones. Leave time to check missing answers. Under the current rules, pocket or scientific calculators are allowed in Upper Primary. Careful reasoning still matters.

Problems can involve number, fractions and ratios, algebra, geometry and measurement, statistics, probability and counting. You'll learn to notice the mathematical structure underneath the story, and choose a method that fits.

Take number patterns. What is the final digit of three to the power of one hundred and twenty-three? The last digits repeat: three, nine, seven, one. Four positions make a cycle.

One hundred and twenty-three is thirty groups of four, with three left over. So use the third position: seven. A remainder turns an enormous calculation into a small decision.

Ratios become clearer as equal parts. Blue and gold counters are in the ratio three to five. There are twelve more gold. The difference is two parts, so each part is six. Altogether, eight parts make forty-eight.

Geometry rewards seeing structure. In a three by four grid, how many rectangles are there? Choose two of the four horizontal lines and two of the five vertical lines. Six choices times ten gives sixty rectangles.

Five is subtracted from a number, the result is doubled, then seven is added, giving thirty-one. Undo the steps in reverse: subtract seven, halve, then add five. The starting number is seventeen.

Counting and probability need organised cases. Roll two fair six-sided dice. Six ordered outcomes add to seven, from thirty-six equally likely outcomes. The probability is one sixth. A table helps you count every case exactly once.

Can fifteen counters split into two equal whole-number groups? Pair them: one is left over. This is parity. Along with colouring and things that stay unchanged, it can rule out impossible answers. You'll learn to explain why.

Each lesson starts with the idea: what it solves, why it helps, and clues that tell you when to use it. A small worked example makes the method visible before the challenge grows.

Five quick A or B choices practise useful decisions. For the rectangle problem, should you count squares only, or choose pairs of boundary lines? Choose the lines. The aim is to recognise the next move, then understand why it works.

Next, you'll practise with support, solve independently, then meet a different-looking problem using the same idea. Explain your plan and check your answer. The numbers and stories change, so you learn methods you can carry into unseen questions.

Mock exams bring the methods together under a timer. Afterwards, review your answers and worked solutions. Notice whether the difficulty was reading, choosing a method, calculating or checking. Repair that skill, then revisit it later.

You don't need every answer today. Start with a clue, draw a picture, try a small case, and build from there. Each thoughtful attempt strengthens your toolkit. Let's begin.

Exam facts and sources

This introduction covers AMC Upper Primary. The examples are teaching demonstrations. Current rules were checked in November 2026; follow your school’s instructions for your sitting.

  • Australian Maths Trust: AMC — divisions, timing, question formats, topics, scoring and incorrect responses.
  • AMT competition handbook — permitted equipment and exam procedures. Pocket or scientific calculators are currently permitted in Upper Primary.

Summit One is an independent training resource. Its lessons develop flexible problem solving; they do not predict future questions or guarantee a score.
