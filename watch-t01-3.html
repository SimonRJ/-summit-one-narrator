T01.3

Use the model to check the answer

Predict what kind of answer makes sense, then test it against every condition.

2:19 · Full HD

Try it in your notebook

Pack 100 eggs into boxes of 12, filling each box before opening the next. How many boxes are needed, and how many eggs go in the last box?

Check your reasoning

100 = 8 × 12 + 4. Use 9 boxes, with 4 eggs in the last box. Eight boxes hold only 96 eggs.

Try a variation

A van carries 8 crates on each trip. It must move 49 crates. How many trips are needed, and how many spaces remain empty on the last trip?

See the worked answer

Six full trips carry 48 crates. The seventh carries 1 crate, leaving 7 of its 8 spaces empty. Six trips cannot carry the whole load.

0:00 Predict the units and size of an answer

Imagine a calculation gives seven and a half people in a queue. A headcount has to be a whole number. That tells you to look back at your reasoning. Start problems by asking what a sensible answer could look like. Then use that idea to check the result.

A count of buses is a whole number. A probability is between zero and one, including both ends. An area has square units. Three metres along each side of a square gives nine square metres. Identify the kind of answer first. The model also helps you predict its size.

0:35 Estimate seats for 130 students

One hundred and thirty students are going on a trip. Each bus has seats for forty students. What is the smallest number of buses that can take the whole group? Make a quick estimate before you continue. Think about what happens if a bus is only partly full.

0:53 Fill buses in groups of forty

Group the students into forties. One bus takes forty. A second takes another forty. A third brings the total on board to one hundred and twenty. There are still ten students waiting. Write one hundred and thirty as three lots of forty plus ten.

Those ten students still need seats. Put them in a fourth bus. Ten of its forty seats are used, so thirty are spare. Everyone now has a seat, and the answer is four buses.

1:22 Show why three buses fall short

Check both sides of the boundary. Four buses make one hundred and sixty seats, enough for all one hundred and thirty students. Three buses make one hundred and twenty seats, so ten students would be left out. Any smaller number has even fewer seats. Four is the smallest number that works.

1:40 Count only the completely full buses

Keep the same trip. This time ask how many buses are completely full if the driver fills each bus before opening the next. Each of the first three has forty students. The fourth has ten. So three buses are full. The last sentence of the question tells you which count to give.
