Choose a strategy, then learn from the decision

Worked example: organise a count, then check it a second way.

1:21 · Full HD

Try it in your notebook

Choose two different whole numbers from 1 to 6 inclusive. How many unordered pairs have a sum of at most 5?

Check your reasoning

Fix the smaller number. With 1, the larger number can be 2, 3 or 4. With 2, only 3 fits. With 3 or more, even the smallest possible partner is too large. That gives four pairs.

Read the transcript

Choose two different numbers from one, two, three, four, five and six. The order does not matter. How many choices have a sum of at least nine?

To avoid counting any pair twice, always name the larger number first. Then organise the count by the larger number.

If the larger number is six, the smaller must be at least three: three, four or five. That is three pairs. If the larger number is five, the smaller must be four: one pair. If the larger is four, the best sum is four plus three, which is only seven. No pairs.

Three plus one is four. The pairs are three and six, four and six, five and six, and four and five.

Check a different way, by the sum. Nine comes from three and six, or four and five. Ten comes from four and six. Eleven comes from five and six. Two plus one plus one is four. Notice that "at least nine" includes nine itself.

Fix an order so nothing is counted twice, sort into cases, then check another way. Rebuild this, then count the pairs from one to six whose sum is at most five.
