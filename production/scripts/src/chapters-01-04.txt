@@ T01.1
GOAL: Attach a meaning and a unit to every quantity before calculating.
[INSIGHT]
V: Three tiles carry 4, 3 and 2. Each number gains its label as a ceramic packet or counter comes into view.
N: When a maths problem gives you three numbers, each number can do a different job. One might count packets. Another might tell you how many counters fit in each packet. The third might count counters on their own. Give every number its job before you calculate.
[WHY]
V: Keep the request visible. Build 4 + 3 + 2 = 9 from the tiles, then lift the different unit labels to expose the invalid addition.
N: Start with the question: how many counters altogether? That tells us the unit of our answer. Four plus three plus two gives nine, but we would be adding a packet count to two different descriptions of counters. Give each quantity its label and ask what it would take to get everything into counters.
[PUZZLE]
V: Four ceramic packets appear in turn, each with three counters visible inside. Two blue loose counters slide in alongside. Hold the complete diagram and the question.
N: Mia has four packets of counters. Each packet contains three counters, and Mia has two loose counters as well. How many counters does she have altogether? Before you work it out, point to what the four, the three and the two each describe.
[DEVELOP]
V: Packet lids lift and fourteen objects retain their identities. The twelve gold counters settle into four rows of three. Build 4 packets × 3 counters per packet = 12 counters.
N: Four tells us how many groups there are. Three tells us the size of every group. Open the first packet and you can see three counters. Open the others and the same group repeats. Four groups of three give twelve counters. Multiplication gives the counter total for the packets.
[DEVELOP]
V: Two loose blue counters slide below the twelve gold counters. Combine the two labelled quantities and outline all fourteen.
N: The two loose counters are already in the unit we want. Slide them into the collection. Now we are adding twelve counters and two counters, and the total is fourteen counters. Check it in the picture: four rows of three, and two beside them.
[VARIATION]
V: Place proportional ribbons of 2 m and 30 cm on a common length scale. Relabel the first as 200 cm and join them end to end to show 230 cm.
N: The same idea works with measurements. One ribbon is two metres long, and another is thirty centimetres long. How much ribbon is there altogether? Two metres is two hundred centimetres. Both lengths are now in centimetres, so together they measure two hundred and thirty centimetres.
[VARIATION]
V: A page-number strip from 95 to 105 builds in two rows. Introduce the question and let the learner scan the full range.
N: A number can have a different job even when it uses the same digits. Think of a book with pages numbered ninety-five through one hundred and five. In just that range, how many times is the digit nine written? Look across the page numbers and find each printed nine.
[VARIATION]
V: Highlight each page containing a 9. Transfer each highlighted glyph to its own position in a second row. Show five pages and six written nines.
N: Five page numbers contain a nine: ninety-five, ninety-six, ninety-seven, ninety-eight and ninety-nine. The last page number on that list contains two nines. Its first nine counts once and its second nine counts once. There are six written nines in all.
[RECAP]
V: Rebuild the three-step method, then show six closed boxes of eight pencils each and the full notebook prompt.
N: Read the final question. Label each number with what it counts or measures. Bring quantities into a common unit when you need to add them. Here is your notebook challenge. Six boxes hold eight pencils each. Five pencils are broken. How many unbroken pencils are there? Label the quantities and show your calculation.
TRY: A shelf holds 6 boxes, each with 8 pencils. Five pencils are broken. How many unbroken pencils are there?
ANS: 6 × 8 = 48 pencils and 48 − 5 = 43 unbroken pencils.

@@ T04.2
GOAL: Count the factors of a number by choosing how many of each prime to use.
[INSIGHT]
V: The number 12 with its six factors 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 appearing in a ring.
N: Twelve has six factors: one, two, three, four, six and twelve. For small numbers you can list them. For large numbers you need a method that counts them without listing. That method comes from prime factors.
[WHY]
V: The number 360 with a long list of factors scrolling past; a counter ticks up to 24.
N: Three hundred and sixty has twenty-four factors. Listing them is slow and it is easy to miss one. Counting choices is fast and cannot miss.
[PUZZLE]
V: 12 = 2 x 2 x 3 as tiles. A grid with columns "use zero 2s, one 2, two 2s" and rows "use zero 3s, one 3". Empty cells. Question: "What goes in each cell?"
N: Twelve is two times two times three. A factor of twelve is made from some of those tiles. Pause and work out how many different selections are possible.
[DEVELOP]
V: The grid fills: top row 1, 2, 4; bottom row 3, 6, 12. Each cell shows the tiles used.
N: For the twos, you may use none, one or two of them: three choices. For the three, you may use it or not: two choices. Three times two is six selections, and each makes a different factor. Using no tiles at all gives the factor one. Using them all gives twelve itself.
[DEVELOP]
V: 72 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 3 x 3. Choice counters: "2s: 0 to 3, four choices", "3s: 0 to 2, three choices". Result 4 x 3 = 12.
N: Try seventy-two. It is two cubed times three squared. There are four choices for how many twos, from zero to three, and three choices for how many threes. Four times three is twelve factors. The rule is: add one to each power, then multiply.
[VARIATION]
V: Factors of 36 pair up: 1 with 36, 2 with 18, 3 with 12, 4 with 9, and 6 alone in the centre.
N: Here is a neat consequence. Factors come in pairs that multiply to the number. For thirty-six: one and thirty-six, two and eighteen, three and twelve, four and nine. But six pairs with itself. That lonely middle factor only happens for square numbers. So square numbers are exactly the numbers with an odd number of factors.
[RECAP]
V: Lines: "Factorise into primes", "Add 1 to each power", "Multiply".
N: Factorise, add one to each power, and multiply. Notebook challenge: how many factors does two hundred have?
TRY: How many factors does 200 have?
ANS: 200 = 2^3 x 5^2, so (3 + 1)(2 + 1) = 12 factors.

@@ T04.3
GOAL: Use prime copies to find highest common factors, lowest common multiples and squares.
[INSIGHT]
V: Two rows of prime tiles: 12 as 2, 2, 3 and 18 as 2, 3, 3. Tiles that both rows share glow.
N: Write two numbers as rows of prime tiles and you can see what they share. Twelve is two, two, three. Eighteen is two, three, three. Comparing the rows, one prime at a time, answers several kinds of question.
[WHY]
V: Three question cards: "largest shared factor?", "smallest shared multiple?", "is it a square?".
N: Highest common factor, lowest common multiple, and perfect squares look like three different topics. They are really one idea: counting how many copies of each prime you have.
[PUZZLE]
V: The two rows for 12 and 18 with a blank row beneath labelled "shared" and another labelled "covers both".
N: What is the largest number that divides both twelve and eighteen? And what is the smallest number that both divide into? Pause and use the tiles.
[DEVELOP]
V: Shared row fills with one 2 and one 3: label 6.
N: A common factor can only use tiles that both numbers have. Both have one two, and both have one three. So the highest common factor is two times three, which is six. For each prime, take the smaller count.
[DEVELOP]
V: "Covers both" row fills with 2, 2, 3, 3: label 36.
N: A common multiple needs enough tiles to cover each number. Twelve needs two twos. Eighteen needs two threes. So the lowest common multiple is two times two times three times three, which is thirty-six. For each prime, take the larger count.
[VARIATION]
V: 12 x 18 = 216 and 6 x 36 = 216 appear together.
N: Notice that six times thirty-six equals twelve times eighteen. For two numbers, the highest common factor times the lowest common multiple always equals their product, because between them they use every tile exactly once.
[VARIATION]
V: 36 as tiles 2, 2, 3, 3 pairing off into two equal groups 2, 3 and 2, 3. Then 48 as 2, 2, 2, 2, 3 with the 3 left unpaired; one extra 3 slides in.
N: Squares are about pairs. Thirty-six splits into two identical groups, so it is a square. Forty-eight is four twos and a three. The three has no partner, so forty-eight is not a square. Multiplying by three fixes that: one hundred and forty-four is twelve squared. A number is a square exactly when every prime appears an even number of times.
[RECAP]
V: Lines: "Common factor: smaller count", "Common multiple: larger count", "Square: every count even".
N: Smaller counts for the highest common factor. Larger counts for the lowest common multiple. Even counts for a square. Notebook challenge: find the highest common factor and lowest common multiple of twenty and thirty.
TRY: Find the highest common factor and lowest common multiple of 20 and 30.
ANS: 20 = 2 x 2 x 5 and 30 = 2 x 3 x 5: HCF 10, LCM 60.

@@ T04.E
GOAL: Worked example: the smallest multiplier that turns 90 into a perfect square.
[PUZZLE]
V: The number 90 beside an empty multiplier box and a target labelled "a perfect square". Question: "What is the smallest positive whole number to put in the box?"
N: What is the smallest positive whole number you can multiply ninety by to make a perfect square? Pause and think about what a square looks like in prime tiles.
[DEVELOP]
V: 90 splits into blue tiles 2, 3, 3, 5. The two 3s move together as a pair; 2 and 5 stand alone.
N: Factorise ninety: two times three times three times five. A square needs every prime in pairs. The threes already form a pair. The two and the five are each alone.
[DEVELOP]
V: Gold tiles 2 and 5 slide in beside their partners. Multiplier box shows 10.
N: Give each lonely prime a partner. We need one more two and one more five. Two times five is ten, so multiply by ten.
[DEVELOP]
V: All tiles split into two identical groups 2, 3, 5 and 2, 3, 5. Label 900 = 30 x 30.
N: Ninety times ten is nine hundred. Its tiles split into two identical groups of two, three, five, and two times three times five is thirty. So nine hundred is thirty squared.
[VARIATION]
V: Trial multipliers 2 and 5 shown failing: with only an extra 2, the 5 is still alone; with only an extra 5, the 2 is still alone.
N: Could a smaller multiplier work? Any multiplier that works must supply a two and a five, so it must be a multiple of ten. Nothing smaller than ten can do that. Ten is the smallest.
[RECAP]
V: Line: "pair every prime". Notebook prompt.
N: To make a square, pair every prime. Rebuild this solution, then find the smallest positive whole number that multiplies sixty to make a perfect square.
TRY: Find the smallest positive whole number that multiplies 60 to make a perfect square.
ANS: 60 = 2 x 2 x 3 x 5, so multiply by 3 x 5 = 15, giving 900.
