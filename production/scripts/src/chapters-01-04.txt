@@ T01.1
GOAL: Attach a meaning and a unit to every number before calculating.
[INSIGHT]
V: Matte dark stage. Three plain number tiles 4, 3, 2 float with no labels. One by one a small tag slides under each: "packets", "counters in each packet", "loose counters".
N: Every number in a maths question has a job. One number might count packets, another might count what is inside each packet, and another might count loose items. Before you calculate anything, give each number a label that says what it counts, and what unit it is measured in.
[WHY]
V: Two worked lines side by side. Left: 4 + 3 + 2 = 9, greyed, with a small cross. Right: the same tiles with their tags. Tags pulse gently.
N: Why bother? Because most wrong answers in competitions are not arithmetic slips. They are correct calculations of the wrong thing. If you add packets to counters, the sum looks tidy, but it means nothing. Labels stop that mistake before it starts.
[PUZZLE]
V: Four ceramic packets appear, each with three counters visible through a window, plus two loose counters. Question text: "How many counters altogether?" Hold two seconds.
N: Here is a small puzzle. Mia has four packets. Each packet holds three counters. She also has two loose counters. How many counters does she have altogether? Pause and decide what each number is doing.
[DEVELOP]
V: The four packets open; their counters slide out into four rows of three. Equation builds: 4 packets x 3 counters per packet = 12 counters.
N: The four counts packets, and the three counts counters in each packet. Packets times counters per packet gives counters. Four times three is twelve counters.
[DEVELOP]
V: The two loose counters join the twelve. Equation: 12 counters + 2 counters = 14 counters. The word "counters" lines up vertically in both terms.
N: The two is already a number of counters, so it can be added directly. Twelve counters plus two counters is fourteen counters. Notice we only added things with the same label.
[VARIATION]
V: New strip of page numbers 95 to 105. The digit 9 highlights wherever it appears. Two counters at the side: "numbers containing a 9" and "9s written".
N: Now a different trap. Pages are numbered from ninety-five to one hundred and five. How many times is the digit nine written? Five page numbers contain a nine: ninety-five to ninety-nine. But ninety-nine contains it twice. So the digit nine is written six times. "Numbers containing a nine" and "nines written" are different jobs, and the question tells you which one it wants.
[RECAP]
V: Three short lines appear in turn: "Read the request", "Label every number", "Only combine matching labels".
N: So here is the method. Read what the answer must describe. Give every number a label and a unit. Then only add or compare quantities whose labels match. In your notebook, try this one: a shelf holds six boxes, each box holds eight pencils, and five pencils are broken. How many pencils are not broken?
TRY: A shelf holds 6 boxes, each with 8 pencils; 5 pencils are broken. How many pencils are not broken?
ANS: 6 x 8 = 48 pencils; 48 - 5 = 43 pencils.

@@ T03.3
GOAL: Use the pigeonhole principle: more objects than boxes forces a shared box.
[INSIGHT]
V: Three open boxes. Four counters drop in one by one; the fourth must land on an occupied box, which glows.
N: Put four counters into three boxes. However you do it, some box ends up with at least two. This simple idea is called the pigeonhole principle, and it can prove that something must happen, even when you cannot say where.
[WHY]
V: A cloud of "all possible choices" with a single line through it reading "guaranteed".
N: Many competition questions ask for a guarantee: what is the smallest number that makes something certain? You cannot check every possibility. The pigeonhole principle handles them all at once. The skill is choosing the right boxes.
[PUZZLE]
V: A drawer silhouette with socks in red, blue, green and black. Question: "How many socks must you take to be sure of a matching pair?"
N: A drawer has many socks in four colours. You take socks out without looking. How many must you take to be certain of having two of the same colour? Pause and think about the unluckiest possible draw.
[DEVELOP]
V: Four boxes labelled by colour. Four socks land one per box. A fifth sock hovers, then drops onto one of them.
N: Make one box for each colour. The unluckiest start is one sock of each colour: four socks, no pair. The fifth sock must share a box with another. So five socks guarantee a pair, and four do not. Notice both halves: five is enough, and four can fail.
[DEVELOP]
V: Boxes relabelled "remainder 0", "remainder 1", "remainder 2". Four numbers 2, 6, 10, 13 drop in; 10 and 13 share a box. Label 13 - 10 = 3.
N: Boxes do not have to be colours. Take any four whole numbers. Sort them by their remainder when divided by three. There are only three remainders, so two of the numbers share one. Their difference is then a multiple of three. That is guaranteed for every choice of four numbers.
[VARIATION]
V: Numbers 1 to 10 arranged as five pairs that add to 11: (1,10), (2,9), (3,8), (4,7), (5,6). Six chosen numbers must fill some pair.

[RECAP]
V: Lines: "Choose the boxes", "Count objects and boxes", "Show the smaller number can fail".

N: Try seventy-two. It is two cubed times three squared. There are four choices for how many twos, from zero to three, and three choices for how many threes. Four times three is twelve factors. The rule is: add one to each power, then multiply.

@@ T04.3
GOAL: Use prime copies to find highest common factors, lowest common multiples and squares.
[INSIGHT]
V: Two rows of prime tiles: 12 as 2, 2, 3 and 18 as 2, 3, 3. Tiles that both rows share glow.
N: Write two numbers as rows of prime tiles and you can see what they share. Twelve is two, two, three. Eighteen is two, three, three. Comparing the rows, one prime at a time, answers several kinds of question.
[WHY]
V: Three question cards: "largest shared factor?", "smallest shared multiple?", "is it a square?".
N: Highest common factor, lowest common multiple, and perfect squares look like three different topics. They are really one idea: counting how many copies of each prime you have.
[PUZZLE]
V: The two rows for 12 and 18 with a blank row beneath labelled "shared" and another labelled "covers both".
N: What is the largest number that divides both twelve and eighteen? And what is the smallest number that both divide into? Pause and use the tiles.
[DEVELOP]
V: Shared row fills with one 2 and one 3: label 6.
N: A common factor can only use tiles that both numbers have. Both have one two, and both have one three. So the highest common factor is two times three, which is six. For each prime, take the smaller count.
[DEVELOP]
V: "Covers both" row fills with 2, 2, 3, 3: label 36.
N: A common multiple needs enough tiles to cover each number. Twelve needs two twos. Eighteen needs two threes. So the lowest common multiple is two times two times three times three, which is thirty-six. For each prime, take the larger count.
[VARIATION]
V: 12 x 18 = 216 and 6 x 36 = 216 appear together.
N: Notice that six times thirty-six equals twelve times eighteen. For two numbers, the highest common factor times the lowest common multiple always equals their product, because between them they use every tile exactly once.
[VARIATION]
V: 36 as tiles 2, 2, 3, 3 pairing off into two equal groups 2, 3 and 2, 3. Then 48 as 2, 2, 2, 2, 3 with the 3 left unpaired; one extra 3 slides in.
N: Squares are about pairs. Thirty-six splits into two identical groups, so it is a square. Forty-eight is four twos and a three. The three has no partner, so forty-eight is not a square. Multiplying by three fixes that: one hundred and forty-four is twelve squared. A number is a square exactly when every prime appears an even number of times.
[RECAP]
V: Lines: "Common factor: smaller count", "Common multiple: larger count", "Square: every count even".
N: Smaller counts for the highest common factor. Larger counts for the lowest common multiple. Even counts for a square. Notebook challenge: find the highest common factor and lowest common multiple of twenty and thirty.
TRY: Find the highest common factor and lowest common multiple of 20 and 30.
ANS: 20 = 2 x 2 x 5 and 30 = 2 x 3 x 5: HCF 10, LCM 60.

N: Could a smaller multiplier work? Any multiplier that works must supply a two and a five, so it must be a multiple of ten. Nothing smaller than ten can do that. Ten is the smallest.
