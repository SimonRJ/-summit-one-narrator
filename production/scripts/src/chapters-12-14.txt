@@ T12.1
GOAL: Find an unknown angle by linking dependable angle facts in a chain.
[INSIGHT]
V: A straight line with a ray rising from it; the two angles are marked and a half-turn arc sweeps 180 degrees. Then two crossing lines with opposite angles glowing in matching colours.
N: Angle problems are solved by chains. You know one angle, a fact gives you the next, another fact gives the one after. The facts are few: a straight line makes one hundred and eighty degrees, a full turn makes three hundred and sixty, and angles opposite each other where two lines cross are equal.
[WHY]
V: A diagram with "not to scale" in the corner and a protractor fading out.
N: Competition diagrams are not drawn to scale, so measuring is useless. What works is knowing a small set of facts and applying them one link at a time, saying which fact you used.
[PUZZLE]
V: Two parallel lines crossed by a slanted line. One angle at the top crossing is marked 65 degrees. An angle at the lower crossing, on the opposite side, is marked with a question mark.
N: Two parallel lines are crossed by a third line. One angle at the top is sixty-five degrees. Find the marked angle at the bottom. Pause and trace how the top crossing relates to the bottom one.
[DEVELOP]
V: The top crossing slides down along the slanted line onto the bottom crossing, landing exactly on it. Matching angles glow: label "corresponding angles".
N: Because the lines are parallel, the top crossing is an exact copy of the bottom one. Slide it down and it fits. So the angle in the matching position at the bottom is also sixty-five degrees. These are called corresponding angles.
[DEVELOP]
V: At the bottom crossing, the 65 degree angle and the marked angle sit side by side on the straight line. 180 minus 65 gives 115.
N: The angle we want sits next to that sixty-five on a straight line. Together they make one hundred and eighty. So the marked angle is one hundred and fifteen degrees. Two facts, two links, and the chain is complete.
[VARIATION]
V: A triangle with a line drawn through its top corner parallel to the base. The two base angles slide up to sit beside the top angle, forming a straight line.
N: The same parallel-line fact proves something famous. Draw a line through the top of a triangle, parallel to the base. The two base angles reappear beside the top angle, and the three sit together on a straight line. So the angles in any triangle add up to one hundred and eighty degrees.
[VARIATION]
V: An isosceles triangle with equal sides ticked and top angle 40. The base angles fill in as 70 and 70.
N: Add one more link for equal sides. In a triangle with two equal sides, the angles opposite them are equal. If the top angle is forty, the other two share one hundred and forty equally: seventy each.
[RECAP]
V: Lines: "Straight line 180, full turn 360", "Parallel lines copy angles", "Triangle 180; equal sides give equal angles".
N: Build a chain. Name the fact for each link. Notebook challenge: a triangle has two equal sides, and one of its two equal angles is fifty-five degrees. What is the third angle?
TRY: An isosceles triangle has equal angles of 55 degrees. Find the third angle.
ANS: 180 - 55 - 55 = 70 degrees.

@@ T12.2
GOAL: Use equal spacing on a circle, and the link between an angle at the centre and one on the circle.
[INSIGHT]
V: A circle with 12 equally spaced points like a clock face. Spokes from the centre show 12 equal angles of 30 degrees.
N: Put twelve points equally spaced round a circle, like the hours on a clock. From the centre, the full turn of three hundred and sixty degrees is shared into twelve equal angles of thirty degrees. Counting gaps between points tells you an angle at the centre.
[WHY]
V: Clock hands, a wheel with spokes, a regular polygon inside a circle.
N: Clock problems, wheels and regular shapes all use this idea. And there is a second fact, about angles drawn from a point on the circle itself, that unlocks many harder problems.
[PUZZLE]
V: The clock circle. Points at 12 and 4 are joined to the centre. Question: "What is the angle at the centre?"
N: On the clock circle, join the centre to the point at twelve and to the point at four. What angle is made at the centre? Pause and count carefully.
[DEVELOP]
V: The four gaps from 12 to 4 light up one by one. 4 times 30 gives 120. A faint "5 points" label appears and is crossed.
N: From twelve to four there are four gaps. Four times thirty is one hundred and twenty degrees. Count the gaps, not the points. There are five points from twelve to four, but only four gaps.
[DEVELOP]
V: A new point at 8 on the far side of the circle. Lines from 8 to 12 and from 8 to 4 form an angle at the edge. The centre angle 120 and the edge angle sit over the same arc. Edge angle labelled 60.
N: Now stand at a point on the circle instead, at eight, and look at the same arc from twelve to four. The angle you see is exactly half the angle at the centre: sixty degrees. An angle on the circle is half the angle at the centre standing on the same arc.
[DEVELOP]
V: The radius from the centre to 8 is drawn, splitting the picture into two isosceles triangles with equal radii ticked. Base angles are marked equal.
N: Why half? Draw the radius to your viewing point. It makes two triangles, each with two equal sides, because all radii are equal. Their equal angles, added up, show that the centre angle is twice the angle at the edge.
[VARIATION]
V: The arc is a semicircle from 12 to 6: centre angle 180. A point at 3 sees it with angle 90.
N: A special case is worth remembering. If the arc is exactly half the circle, the angle at the centre is one hundred and eighty, so the angle on the circle is ninety. Any triangle drawn with a diameter as one side, and its third corner on the circle, has a right angle.
[RECAP]
V: Lines: "Equal spacing: 360 shared equally", "Count gaps, not points", "Angle on the circle is half the angle at the centre".
N: Share three hundred and sixty among the gaps. Count gaps, not points. The angle on the circle is half the angle at the centre. Notebook challenge: on the clock circle, what is the angle at the centre between the points at two and seven?
TRY: On a clock circle, what is the angle at the centre between the points at 2 and 7?
ANS: 5 gaps x 30 = 150 degrees.

@@ T13.1
GOAL: Find areas using a base with its perpendicular height, and by subtracting simpler regions.
[INSIGHT]
V: A triangle. A second identical copy rotates and joins it to make a parallelogram, which then shears into a rectangle of the same base and height.
N: Two identical triangles fit together to make a parallelogram, and a parallelogram has the same area as a rectangle with the same base and height. So a triangle's area is half its base times its height. The height must be measured straight up from the base, at a right angle.
[WHY]
V: A triangle with its slanted side highlighted and crossed, then the perpendicular height highlighted and ticked.
N: The most common error is using a slanted side as the height. The second most common is trying to measure an awkward shape directly, when it would be far easier to subtract simple shapes from a rectangle.
[PUZZLE]
V: A base of length 8. Three triangles on that base with top corners at different points along a line parallel to the base, 5 above it. Question: "Which triangle has the largest area?"
N: Three triangles share a base of eight centimetres. Their top corners lie at different places along a line parallel to the base, five centimetres above it. Which triangle has the largest area? Pause and think about the height of each.
[DEVELOP]
V: A perpendicular height of 5 is drawn for each triangle, one falling outside the leaning triangle. Each area reads 20.
N: Every one of them has height five, because the top corners are all five centimetres above the base. Half of eight times five is twenty. All three have the same area: twenty square centimetres. Sliding the top corner along a parallel line changes the shape, but not the area.
[DEVELOP]
V: A 6 by 4 rectangle with a slanted triangle inside whose corners are on the rectangle's sides at awkward positions: corners at bottom-left, at a point on the right side 3 up, and at a point on the top 2 from the left. Three right-angled triangles fill the space outside it.
N: Now an awkward triangle inside a six by four rectangle. Its sides are all slanted. Do not attack it directly. The rectangle has area twenty-four. Around the triangle are three right-angled triangles, with areas nine, four and two.
[DEVELOP]
V: The three outer triangles lift away with their areas: 9, 4, 2. Remaining area 24 minus 15 gives 9.
N: Those three take up fifteen. Twenty-four minus fifteen leaves nine. The awkward triangle has area nine square centimetres. Subtracting the surroundings is called using the complement.
[VARIATION]
V: A rectangle with one diagonal. The two halves are identical. Then a point anywhere on the top side joined to the two bottom corners: the triangle is still half the rectangle.
N: One more useful fact. Join any point on the top edge of a rectangle to both bottom corners. The triangle has the rectangle's base and the rectangle's height, so it is always exactly half the rectangle, wherever the point is.
[RECAP]
V: Lines: "Half base times perpendicular height", "Same base and height: same area", "Awkward shape: subtract from a rectangle".
N: Half base times perpendicular height. Triangles with the same base and height have the same area. For awkward shapes, subtract from a rectangle. Notebook challenge: a triangle has base ten centimetres and area thirty-five square centimetres. What is its height?
TRY: A triangle has base 10 cm and area 35 square cm. Find its height.
ANS: Half of 10 times height is 35, so the height is 7 cm.

@@ T13.2
GOAL: Tell apart changes to a boundary and changes of scale.
[INSIGHT]
V: A rectangle. A square bite is taken from a corner: the outline's length does not change. Then a bite from the middle of a side: the outline gets longer.
N: Cutting a piece out of a shape always reduces its area. But the perimeter might stay the same, or even grow. Area and perimeter respond to changes in completely different ways.
[WHY]
V: Two shapes with equal area and very different perimeters side by side.
N: Questions often ask about perimeter after a cut, or area after an enlargement. Each needs its own reasoning, and mixing them up is a common mistake.
[PUZZLE]
V: A 5 by 3 rectangle with a 1 by 1 square removed from a corner. Question: "What is the new perimeter?"
N: A five by three rectangle has a one by one square cut from a corner. What is its perimeter now? Pause and compare the old boundary with the new.
[DEVELOP]
V: The original outline: perimeter 16. At the cut corner, two outer segments of length 1 are removed and two inner segments of length 1 appear; the inner segments slide outward to sit where the old ones were.
N: The original perimeter is sixteen. The cut removes two short pieces of boundary, each of length one. But it exposes two new pieces, also each of length one. The perimeter is still sixteen. The new edges can be slid out to replace the old ones exactly.
[DEVELOP]
V: A fresh 5 by 3 rectangle with a 1 by 1 notch cut from the middle of a long side. One segment of length 1 is removed; three segments of length 1 appear. Perimeter 18.
N: Now cut the same square from the middle of a side instead. One piece of length one is removed, but three new pieces appear. The perimeter rises by two, to eighteen. Same area removed, different effect on the boundary.
[VARIATION]
V: A 2 by 3 rectangle beside a copy with every length doubled: 4 by 6. Perimeters 10 and 20. The large one is tiled by four copies of the small one: areas 6 and 24.
N: Scaling is a different kind of change. Double every length of a two by three rectangle and you get four by six. The perimeter doubles, from ten to twenty. But the area becomes four times as big, from six to twenty-four, because both the length and the width were doubled.
[VARIATION]
V: A unit cube beside a cube of side 2 made from 8 unit cubes; then a cube of side 3 made from 27.
N: For solids, three directions are scaled. Doubling every length of a cube makes its volume eight times as large. Tripling makes it twenty-seven times as large.
[RECAP]
V: Lines: "Cuts: compare removed and new boundary", "Scale by k: lengths times k, areas times k squared, volumes times k cubed".
N: For a cut, compare the boundary removed with the boundary created. For scaling by a factor, lengths multiply by the factor, areas by its square, and volumes by its cube. Notebook challenge: a photograph is enlarged so that every length is three times as long. How many times as large is its area?
TRY: Every length of a photograph is tripled. How many times as large is its area?
ANS: 3 x 3 = 9 times.
