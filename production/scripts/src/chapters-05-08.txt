@@ T05.1
GOAL: Decide whether swapping two chosen objects makes a new outcome.
[INSIGHT]
V: Three ceramic counters A, B, C. Two podium places "1st" and "2nd" on the left; a plain tray labelled "team of 2" on the right.
N: Before counting anything, ask one question: does order matter? Choosing a winner and a runner-up is different from choosing a team of two. On the podium, swapping the two people changes the result. In a team, swapping them changes nothing.
[WHY]
V: Two answers, 6 and 3, hover over the same three counters with a question mark between them.
N: Getting this wrong doubles your answer, or halves it. It is one of the most common counting mistakes, and the cure is to decide about order before you start multiplying.
[PUZZLE]
V: Counters A, B, C. Question one: "How many ways to fill 1st and 2nd?" Question two: "How many teams of two?"
N: Three runners, Ava, Ben and Cal, are in a race. In how many ways can first and second place be filled? And how many different pairs of them could form a team? Pause and try both.
[DEVELOP]
V: Six ordered pairs appear in two columns: AB, AC, BA, BC, CA, CB. Count 3 times 2.
N: For the podium, there are three choices for first place, then two remaining choices for second. Three times two is six ordered results.
[DEVELOP]
V: The six ordered pairs slide together in twos: AB with BA, AC with CA, BC with CB. Three merged tiles remain.
N: For a team, Ava then Ben is the same team as Ben then Ava. The six ordered results merge in pairs, leaving three teams. We divide by two because each team was counted in two orders.
[VARIATION]
V: Five counters. A row of three places gives 5, 4, 3. Then groups of six orderings collapse into one tile each; ten tiles remain.
N: Now choose three people from five. In order, that is five times four times three, which is sixty. As an unordered group, each set of three people can be arranged in six orders, so sixty divided by six gives ten groups. The number you divide by is the number of ways to arrange the chosen objects.
[VARIATION]
V: A team of three with role badges "captain", "scorer", "timekeeper" appearing on the counters.
N: Read the wording carefully. If the three chosen people get different jobs, such as captain, scorer and timekeeper, then order matters again and the answer is sixty.
[RECAP]
V: Lines: "Different roles or places: order matters", "Just a group: divide by the arrangements".
N: If positions or roles differ, count in order. If only membership matters, divide by the number of arrangements of the chosen group. Notebook challenge: from six players, how many ways are there to choose a captain and a vice-captain? And how many ways to choose two players to carry the equipment?
TRY: From 6 players: how many ways to choose a captain and vice-captain? How many ways to choose 2 equipment carriers?
ANS: 6 x 5 = 30 with roles; 30 / 2 = 15 as an unordered pair.

@@ T05.2
GOAL: Split a count into cases using the restriction that changes the number of choices.
[INSIGHT]
V: A tangled set of outcomes separates into three clean trays with no overlap.
N: Some counting problems are too awkward to do in one go. The trick is to split them into cases. Good cases have two properties: no outcome belongs to two cases, and every outcome belongs to one.
[WHY]
V: A product 4 x 4 x 3 with a warning mark, next to two smaller tidy products.
N: Multiplying choices only works when the number of choices does not depend on what you chose earlier. When a restriction changes the choices, a single multiplication breaks. Splitting at the restriction repairs it.
[PUZZLE]
V: Digit tiles 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. Three empty places. Question: "How many even three-digit numbers with no repeated digit?"
N: Using the digits zero, one, two, three and four, with no digit repeated, how many even three-digit numbers can be made? Pause and think about which place causes the trouble.
[DEVELOP]
V: The last place highlights with options 0, 2, 4. The 0 option separates from 2 and 4.
N: The number must be even, so the last digit is zero, two or four. But zero is special, because a number cannot start with zero. So split by the last digit: either it is zero, or it is two or four.
[DEVELOP]
V: Case 1: last digit 0; first place 4 choices, middle 3 choices: 12. Case 2: last digit 2 or 4; first place 3 choices (not 0, not the last digit), middle 3 choices: 2 x 3 x 3 = 18.
N: If the last digit is zero, the first digit has four choices and the middle has three: twelve numbers. If the last digit is two or four, the first digit cannot be zero or the last digit, leaving three choices, and the middle has three. That is two times three times three: eighteen numbers.
[DEVELOP]
V: The two trays combine: 12 and 18 make 30.
N: The cases cannot overlap, because a number has only one last digit. Add them: twelve plus eighteen is thirty.
[VARIATION]
V: Two switches, each on or off: four outcomes in a grid. The outcome "on, on" is crossed out. Three remain.
N: Sometimes it is quicker to count what is forbidden. Two switches have four settings altogether. If "both on" is not allowed, subtract that one, leaving three. Counting everything and subtracting the unwanted part is called using the complement.
[RECAP]
V: Lines: "Split at the restriction", "Multiply inside a case", "Add separate cases".
N: Split at the feature that changes the choices. Multiply within a case. Add the cases. Notebook challenge: how many two-digit numbers have two different digits?
TRY: How many two-digit numbers have two different digits?
ANS: 90 two-digit numbers minus 9 with equal digits (11, 22, ..., 99) = 81.

[DEVELOP]
V: A single line is highlighted, with a "1" at each end. The total 30 halves to 15 as all fifteen lines are drawn.
