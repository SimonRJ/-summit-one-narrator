Place-value decomposition and estimation

Welcome to Place Value and Estimation. Large numbers need not mean long calculations. When you see a number close to a tidy round number, you can take it apart, work with easier pieces, and put it back together exactly. Let's see an easy, medium, and hard example.

Easy problem. Work out three hundred and ninety-eight plus two hundred and seven, without a calculator. First estimate: four hundred plus two hundred is about six hundred. That tells us the size of a sensible answer before we calculate.

Write three hundred and ninety-eight as four hundred minus two. Write two hundred and seven as two hundred plus seven. Together that is six hundred, plus five, giving six hundred and five. Estimation checks the scale; careful adjustment gives the exact result.

Medium problem. Find fifty-seven times ninety-eight. Ninety-eight is close to one hundred, so imagine a rectangle fifty-seven units high and one hundred units wide. Then remove a strip only two units wide. This picture is an equation waiting to happen.

The large rectangle has area fifty-seven times one hundred, which is five thousand seven hundred. The removed strip has area fifty-seven times two, which is one hundred and fourteen. Subtracting gives five thousand, five hundred and eighty-six. That is close to our estimate, not a huge mysterious product.

Here is the general habit. Look for a useful round number. Replace the awkward part by a tidy quantity plus or minus a small adjustment. Keep that adjustment visible. The picture, the words and the equation should all describe the same relationship.

Hard problem. Which is greater: forty-nine thousand, nine hundred and ninety-six times fifty thousand and four, or fifty thousand squared minus seventeen? Do not multiply out these enormous numbers. Look at how far each factor is from fifty thousand.

The factors are fifty thousand minus four and fifty thousand plus four. The extra strips cancel, leaving fifty thousand squared minus four squared. So the first expression is fifty thousand squared minus sixteen. The second is fifty thousand squared minus seventeen. Therefore the product is greater by exactly one. A difference of squares replaces a mountain of arithmetic.

Easy, medium, hard: compensation, an area model, and a hidden identity. Each uses the same idea: break a number around a convenient value, then account for the small change. Now it's your turn. Work through the next lesson problems and explain why each adjustment is valid.
