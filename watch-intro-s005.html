Welcome to digit sums and counting digits in ranges. Today a place-value column has two jobs. It tells us the value of a digit, and it gives us a position where a digit can appear. We will use both ideas in one harder puzzle.

In AMC practice, a question might ask how many times a digit is written, or which numbers pass a divisibility test. A Mawa style investigation can ask what changes when you move the end of the range. A column-by-column count makes the pattern easier to see.

Is five thousand seven hundred and twenty-four a multiple of nine? Add its four digits. Before we use the answer, think about why it is safe to replace a thousand, a hundred or a ten by a single unit in this test.

Ten, one hundred and one thousand are each one more than a multiple of nine. For this test each place-value unit contributes the same remainder as one. Five plus seven plus two plus four is eighteen. Eighteen is a multiple of nine, so the original number is too.

Write the numbers from one through sixty-five. How many times would you write the digit four? Choose whether you are counting numbers or actual marks on the page. Which number in the range might make those answers different?

First watch the units column. Four, fourteen, twenty-four, and so on up to sixty-four give seven marks. In the tens column, forty through forty-nine give ten more. Seven plus ten is seventeen. The two fours in forty-four are in two different places, and both count.

Now write only the multiples of nine from one through two hundred and fifty. How many times would you write the digit two? Keep the columns separate, and use the divisibility rule to decide which numbers are allowed.

Start with units. The first multiple of nine ending in two is seventy-two. The units cycle repeats after ten multiples, so adding ninety gives one hundred and sixty-two. The next is two hundred and fifty-two, outside our range. That is two marks in the units column.

In the tens column, check the twenties, the one hundred and twenties, and the two hundred and twenties. The digit sums give twenty-seven, one hundred and twenty-six, and two hundred and twenty-five. Three tens marks. Each of those ten-number bands contains just one permitted number with two in the tens place.

In the hundreds column, start at two hundred and seven and count by nines. We get two hundred and sixteen, two hundred and twenty-five, two hundred and thirty-four, and two hundred and forty-three. Five hundreds marks. Two plus three plus five means ten written twos.

Two hundred and twenty-five contributes once in the tens and once in the hundreds. Counting it only once would answer how many numbers contain a two. Another trap is including two hundred and fifty-two. It passes the divisibility test, but the range ends at two hundred and fifty.

On an exam, underline times the digit is written or how many numbers. Draw one tally line per position, write the range endpoints, and apply any filter before adding. Then check one number with a repeat digit to make sure you have counted the right thing.
